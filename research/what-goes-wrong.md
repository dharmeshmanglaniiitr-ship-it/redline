# What Goes Wrong: Recurring Clause Types That Burn People

Pre-PRD research for Redline. Goal: find real-world evidence of which clause types
actually cause pain (money lost, rights lost, disputes, regulatory action), and a rough
directional ranking of how often each shows up in the evidence gathered. This is not a
formal statistic — it reflects what surfaced across 10 web searches and 6 page fetches.

---

## Ranked List (most to least evidenced)

### 1. Auto-renewal / hard-to-cancel subscription clauses (strongest evidence)

This was the single best-evidenced category — it has an actual $150M federal enforcement
action behind it, plus tens of thousands of annual consumer complaints and forum threads
of individuals stuck in contracts.

- **FTC v. Adobe (2024)**: The FTC sued Adobe and two executives, alleging Adobe
  "pre-selected the option for its 'annual paid monthly' plan" without adequately
  disclosing the year-long commitment, then hit customers who tried to cancel early with
  "a hefty early termination fee (which was hidden during the signup process)." The FTC
  also said "the fee, along with other hurdles Adobe uses in its cancellation processes,
  made it difficult to cancel the subscription," and some customers who believed they had
  cancelled were charged anyway. Adobe later agreed to pay $150 million to resolve the
  claims.
  Source: https://consumer.ftc.gov/consumer-alerts/2024/05/adobe-used-hidden-fee-trap-people-paying-subscription-plans-ftc-says
  Related: https://www.lawcommentary.com/articles/adobe-to-pay-150-million-to-resolve-federal-claims-over-hidden-subscription-termination-fees

- **FTC "Click-to-Cancel" rulemaking**: The FTC states it "receives thousands of
  complaints about negative option and recurring subscription practices each year," with
  complaint volume rising to nearly 70/day in 2024 (up from 42/day in 2021), and "more
  than 100,000 complaints in the past five years about subscription services and related
  practices." This directly motivated the FTC's 2024 final rule requiring cancellation to
  be as easy as signup.
  Source: https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2024/10/federal-trade-commission-announces-final-click-cancel-rule-making-it-easier-consumers-end-recurring

- **Individual consumer complaint thread**: A UK consumer forum thread titled "Struggling
  to cancel and auto renewal contract" shows an individual consumer actively seeking help
  after getting stuck in an auto-renewing contract — illustrating that this is not just a
  regulatory abstraction but something ordinary people hit and don't know how to escape.
  Source: https://legalbeagles.info/forums/forum/legalbeagles-consumer-forums/welcome-forum/1667522-struggling-to-cancel-and-auto-renewal-contract

**Why it ranks #1**: it's the only category in this research with a completed nine-figure
federal enforcement action attached, plus a dedicated new federal rule, plus direct
evidence of five-figure-per-day complaint volume.

### 2. Arbitration clauses / class-action waivers

Very well documented at the systemic/statistical level, though less about individual
one-off "I got burned" anecdotes and more about "the system is stacked against you."

- **Prevalence**: "81 companies in the Fortune 100... use arbitration agreements in
  connection with consumer transactions, and of those eighty-one companies, seventy-eight
  include class waivers." Also: "at least 826 million consumer arbitration agreements
  were active in 2018," and "over 60% of U.S. retail e-commerce sales are covered by
  these agreements." In employment, clause use rose from 37.2% of companies (2017) to
  48.9% (2018), covering over 60 million American workers, nearly 25 million of whom also
  waived class-action rights.
  Source: https://centerjd.org/content/fact-sheet-forced-arbitration-clauses-and-class-actions-waivers-numbers

- **Outcome disparity for people who do pursue claims**: the same fact sheet cites CFPB
  data that consumers filed only 411 arbitration cases/year (2010–2012) across major
  financial products despite tens of millions being bound by the clauses, while class
  actions in the same period delivered "$2 billion" in relief to "350 million consumers."
  It also cites the Economic Policy Institute: consumers win relief in only 9% of
  arbitration disputes, and when companies counterclaim, arbitrators side with the
  company 93% of the time.
  Source: https://centerjd.org/content/fact-sheet-forced-arbitration-clauses-and-class-actions-waivers-numbers

- **Employment/civil-rights impact**: "As more companies use forced arbitration clauses
  and class action waivers, many employees are walking away from harassment,
  wrongful-termination and discrimination claims rather than taking them to a privately
  run tribunal."
  Source: https://www.impactfund.org/legal-practitioner-blog/toothless-rights

**Why it ranks #2**: enormous reach (hundreds of millions of agreements) and quantified
harm (win-rate and relief-value disparities), but the evidence found here is
aggregate/statistical rather than many discrete personal complaint stories.

### 3. Data / privacy clauses (data sharing, sale, or use beyond stated purpose)

Multiple distinct, named lawsuits — a strong signal this is a recurring real complaint,
not a one-off.

- **T-Mobile**: sued after it "collected geolocation data from millions of customers and
  sold it to third parties without consent," in a class action; T-Mobile's own defense
  reportedly invoked its own terms of service against the plaintiffs.
  Source: https://gizmodo.com/t-mobile-says-that-getting-sued-for-selling-users-loca-1836247728

- **Flo Health (period-tracking app)**: sued in 2021 following a 2019 Wall Street Journal
  investigation; "although Flo Health promised not to share sensitive health data with
  third parties unless necessary for services, the terms of service actually allowed
  third parties to use the data for their own purposes unrelated to the app." A 2025
  follow-up report found Meta accessed women's health data from the Flo app without
  consent per a court finding.
  Source: https://www.malwarebytes.com/blog/news/2025/08/meta-accessed-womens-health-data-from-flo-app-without-consent-says-court

- **Policygenius**: sued for collecting prospective clients' data and sharing it with
  Facebook and Google via embedded web-tracking technology without adequate consent.
  Source: https://thelyonfirm.com/class-action/data-privacy/

- **Google**: a separate class action claims Google's privacy controls are deceptive and
  "do not stop Google from collecting data regardless of consumer choices."
  Source: https://www.searchenginejournal.com/google-privacy-lawsuit-android-apps/374952/

**Why it ranks #3**: four distinct, named, sourced lawsuits in a single search — strong
recurrence signal, and it maps cleanly onto "the document says one thing, the actual
practice/clause allows another," which is exactly the kind of gap a plain-English
summarizer could catch.

### 4. IP assignment / content-rights / "we may use your content" clauses

The best single piece of evidence here is a very large, very public, very recent
real-world backlash — not a lawsuit, but a demonstrated mass reaction showing this class
of clause genuinely alarms non-lawyers when they encounter it.

- **Adobe's June 2024 Terms of Use update**: sparked "widespread concern among creators
  who feared their work might be used to train AI models," over language that Adobe could
  "access, view, or listen to user content through automated and manual methods to
  improve services using machine learning." One artist's tweet — "I can't use Photoshop
  until I am comfortable with you having unrestricted access to everything I created with
  it, including NDA projects?" — reportedly drew over 5 million views and 50,000 likes,
  and the affected user base was described as "over 20 million global users." Adobe was
  forced to issue clarifications stating "We don't train generative AI on customer
  content."
  Source: https://www.techradar.com/pro/adobe-users-are-furious-about-the-companys-terms-of-service-change-to-help-it-train-ai
  Related: https://venturebeat.com/technology/adobe-tries-again-to-quell-terms-of-service-controversy-we-dont-train-gen-ai-on-customer-content
  Related: https://www.axios.com/2024/06/18/adobe-ai-customer-data-training

- **Freelancer IP-assignment risk (advisory, not a specific complaint)**: multiple
  contract-guide sites describe the underlying mechanism that makes this painful for
  freelancers specifically — e.g. "unless the freelancer contract includes a 'Work for
  Hire,' 'At-Will,' or IP assignment clause, the freelancer may retain rights to the
  work," and conversely that broad assignment language can permanently transfer rights
  the freelancer didn't realize they were giving away.
  Source: https://flag.red/contract-guides/ip-assignment-in-independent-contractor-agreement

**Why it ranks #4**: the Adobe example is arguably the single most viral piece of evidence
found in this whole research pass (millions of views on a real complaint), but it's one
incident rather than many independent complaint threads, and the freelancer-specific
material found was advisory rather than sourced individual complaints.

### 5. Fee escalator / price-increase clauses

Good quantitative evidence of prevalence plus at least one real person asking for help
after being hit by one.

- **Prevalence/scale**: "A 2023 survey by Zuora found that 57% of B2B SaaS companies had
  raised prices in the prior 12 months," and "Gartner data indicates SaaS vendors are
  increasing prices by 9% to 25% while corporate IT budgets grow at only 2.8%." The same
  source notes "built-in escalation clauses ranging from 3% to 10% are standard practice
  across the industry" and that vendors like Salesforce, Microsoft, and ServiceNow have
  normalized annual/CPI-linked price escalators in enterprise agreements.
  Source: https://bunny.com/blog/inflationary-increases-in-saas-contracts-great-idea-hard-reality/

- **Individual complaint/question**: a forum thread titled "Please help with can they
  raise prices mid-contract?" on a legal-questions forum shows a real person seeking help
  after a SaaS vendor apparently changed terms of service to raise prices mid-contract.
  Source: https://terms.law/forum/thread/saas-vendor-changed-tos-raise-prices-midcontract.html

**Why it ranks #5**: solid quantitative prevalence data and at least one direct
individual complaint, but fewer distinct named disputes/lawsuits than categories 1–4.

### 6. Non-compete clauses

Strong at the policy/regulatory-attention level (an entire FTC rulemaking, congressional
hearing with worker testimony) though the specific personal narrative detail was harder
to pull directly from search snippets within budget.

- **Congressional testimony**: at a July 2024 Senate Banking Subcommittee hearing on the
  FTC's non-compete ban, Senator Warren "questioned three witnesses on their personal
  experiences with noncompete clauses preventing them from practicing their trade,"
  including Dr. R. James Toussaint, a specialized orthopedic surgeon who described being
  bound by a non-compete after his practice was acquired by a private equity firm.
  Source: https://www.warren.senate.gov/newsroom/press-releases/icymi-at-hearing-warren-slams-noncompete-agreements-as-unfair-un-american-and-anti-competitive

- **Regulatory fight itself is evidence of scale of the problem**: the FTC's proposed
  nationwide non-compete ban ("which hurt workers and harm competition" per the FTC's own
  framing) was blocked by a federal judge in Texas in mid-2024, showing this is contested
  at a national-policy level, not a fringe issue.
  Source: https://www.npr.org/2024/07/03/nx-s1-5020525/noncompete-ban-block-ftc-competition-ryan-texas
  Source: https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2023/01/ftc-proposes-rule-ban-noncompete-clauses-which-hurt-workers-harm-competition

**Why it ranks #6**: real, high-profile, and policy-relevant, but the evidence gathered
here skews toward "there is a big political fight about this" more than a large volume of
individually sourced personal complaint threads (a next research pass should target
r/legaladvice or news profiles of specific non-compete plaintiffs).

### 7. Indemnity clauses (especially one-sided indemnity in freelance/contractor agreements)

Weaker but real — one direct real-person question, plus advisory content confirming this
is a known freelancer pain point.

- **Real individual question**: a person asked a public legal Q&A site "Do I need an
  indemnity clause in a freelance contract?" — a direct, real, sourced instance of
  someone confused/worried about this clause type before signing.
  Source: https://www.avvo.com/legal-answers/do-i-need-an-indemnity-clause-in-a-freelance-contr-5687954.html

- **Advisory confirmation of the pattern**: freelance red-flag guides explicitly call out
  one-sided indemnity as a recurring problem — "one-sided indemnity clauses where you're
  liable for damages but the client isn't are unacceptable, as mutual indemnification is
  the norm" — implying this asymmetric version shows up often enough to warrant standard
  advice against it.
  Source: https://nobullshitfreelancing.substack.com/p/10-red-flags-to-look-out-for-in-potential

**Why it ranks #7**: real but thinner — one direct sourced individual complaint/question,
reinforced by advisory content rather than multiple independent lawsuits or forum
threads.

### 8. Liability cap clauses (uncapped/unlimited liability exposure)

Weakest of the categories with a source — found mainly as risk-education content rather
than sourced complaints from people who were actually burned.

- Advisory sources describe the mechanism clearly: "Unlimited liability in freelance
  contracts means you could be held responsible for any and all damages, losses, or
  claims arising from your work — without any financial cap," and recommend capping
  liability "at 1x the total fees paid under the contract" as the market-standard
  mitigation — implying the unmitigated version is a known, recurring risk in the
  freelance/contractor market, even though this research did not surface a specific named
  individual or company incident.
  Source: https://flag.red/contract-guides/unlimited-liability-for-freelancers

**Why it ranks #8**: plausible and logically adjacent to indemnity, but the only sourced
material found was general risk-education content, not a specific complaint, lawsuit, or
forum thread of someone actually harmed — this is the weakest-evidenced category in the
report and should be treated as thin evidence, not a top clause type, unless a follow-up
search finds a sourced individual case.

---

### 9. Unilateral termination clauses ("termination for convenience")

Documented as a real, powerful, one-sided contractual tool, mostly in a
construction/government-contracting context; no consumer-forum or individual small-
business complaint was found within budget.

- A construction-law firm blog describes "termination for convenience" as "a powerful
  weapon in contractual disputes," noting a unilateral version is "when the contract
  termination rights are granted to only one party," most commonly used by a general
  contractor to drop a subcontractor "if the general contractor finds a cheaper
  subcontractor to do the same work" — and that the terminated party's main recourse is
  negotiating reimbursement or, in government contracts, filing a claim under the
  Contract Disputes Act.
  Source: https://www.klconstructionlawblog.com/2007/12/01/the-termination-for-convenience-clause-a-powerful-weapon-in-contractual-disputes/

**Why it ranks #9**: the underlying mechanism (one party can walk away at will, other
party bears the risk) is clearly documented, but this research did not find a sourced
consumer/freelancer complaint story — only law-firm explainer content — so treat this as
a plausible category needing more targeted follow-up search (e.g., specific freelance
platform terms, specific gig-worker deactivation stories) rather than as strongly
evidenced as categories 1–5.
